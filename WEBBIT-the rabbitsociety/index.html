Webbit

Webbit is an experimental visual rabbit-ear artwork inspired by human ear wiggling. An invisible two-sided rig reads eyebrow signals with MediaPipe and transfers them to a pair of anchored rabbit ears. A silvery rabbit-in-the-moon brightens with their movement.

How to play

  1. Choose Start ear calibration and allow the camera. Use Chrome or Edge on HTTPS/localhost.
  2. Sit facing the camera in even light. Tuck hair behind your ears and stay fairly close.
  3. Calibration begins automatically. Relax your face for 2 seconds, then raise and relax your left eyebrow, followed by your right eyebrow. The big illustrated scanner highlights the current side; it is a visual metaphor for eyebrow-based calibration.
  4. After calibration, move your eyebrows or naturally wiggle your ears. Increase left/right gain for a larger response, or recalibrate.
  5. If the wrong ear moves, tick Swap left/right.
  6. Both ears are rooted at the bottom of the screen. Their bases stay still, while the upper cartilage and tips flex. You can also use the manual sliders.
  7. Pick a color theme: white & pink, gray & pinkish, or black & yellow.

Behind the trick: MediaPipe does not detect the auricular muscles or directly track your outer ears. This is an expressive proxy rig, not a validated ear-motion sensor. A separate synthetic 3D ear-tip rig is driven by measurements around each eyebrow, and rendered as glossy jelly-like rabbit ears. Nothing is attached to, or follows, your head position. Eyebrow movements can deliberately trigger it. For best results, keep your head fairly still and recalibrate when lighting changes.

Why is ear wiggling unusual?

Most people never learn to move their auricular muscles on command.
  • Why unusual: humans retain anterior, superior, and posterior auricular muscles around the outer ear. Compared with many mammals, our ears have less need to pivot toward sound; conscious control of these muscles is often weak or unpracticed.
  • How rare: there is no single well-established global prevalence estimate; control varies between people, and moving one side independently can be particularly difficult.
  • Surprising leftover: studies show that auricular muscles can show subtle responses linked to auditory attention, even when visible ear movement is minimal.
  • Learning it: some people gain control with practice or biofeedback, though it doesn’t come naturally to most.
